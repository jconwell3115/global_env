Welcoming the Stranger—A Call to Christlike Compassion

Scripture Reading: Matthew 25:31–46

In today’s climate, the idea of welcoming the stranger is a challenging one—perhaps more radical than ever before. Many of us find comfort in the familiar, drawn to protect what’s ours and unsure how to respond when someone different enters our world. For some, national and political loyalties can even eclipse the simple command of Jesus: “I was a stranger and you welcomed me.” 

But Christ’s teachings in Matthew 25 cannot simply be explained away. He paints a dramatic scene where the Son of Man returns in glory and gathers the nations before Him. With powerful clarity, Jesus describes how people are separated—sheep to His right, goats to His left—based on their compassion and response to those in need. “For I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me.” His judgment hinges on practical acts of mercy: feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, visiting the sick and imprisoned, and crucially, embracing the stranger among us[1].

This is not an abstract parable but a searing call to action, rooted deeply in the fabric of the Old Testament. Leviticus admonishes, “You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt: I am the Lord your God” (Leviticus 19:33–34)[2]. God’s people are reminded, again and again, of their own vulnerability—their history of being outsiders, and God’s mercy to them. This memory is meant to shape how they extend grace in their own communities[1].

Yet, it’s easy to draw lines between “us” and “them,” convinced we are adhering to Christian values while perhaps missing the heart of Christ’s command. Political movements, like those rallying under “Make America Great Again,” often claim to act as protectors of Christian faith and culture. But, in practice, these movements have chosen exclusion, border fortification, and harsh rhetoric toward outsiders and immigrants over hospitality and mercy[3]. Some even support actions that separate families, detain the undocumented, or turn away those fleeing danger—all in the name of national security or sovereignty. Here, Christ’s words from Matthew 25 become a poignant reflection—not just a standard to aspire to, but a lens by which our actions and policies are judged.

The challenge for the Church is clear: to live lives marked not by fear or division, but by grace and hospitality. Welcoming the stranger is not a suggestion or a political bargaining chip, but God’s call to His people. It asks for a posture of humility, for an honest recognition that every person—regardless of nationality, status, or language—bears the image of God[2][1]. To invite someone in, to meet their immediate needs, is to honor Christ. Jesus declares, “Truly I say to you, to the extent that you did it to one of these brothers of mine... you did it to me” (Matthew 25:40).

Of course, such radical welcome demands courage. It pushes past comfort zones. At times, extending compassion may feel risky or unpopular. And even the best-intentioned believers may struggle with how to reconcile conviction for law, security, and justice with the call to mercy. Romans 13 reminds us to respect governing authorities. But Paul first and foremost sets the rule of love: “Therefore welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God” (Romans 15:7)[2]. Biblical justice is never merely the enforcement of statutes, but the pursuit of kindness and inclusion—even when it disrupts patterns of power or comfort[1].

For those wrestling with what it means to live faithfully, consider this: When decisions about strangers and immigrants arise—whether through policy, conversation, or daily interaction—ask, “How does my response reflect Christ’s love?” The answer lies not in political talking points, but in the humble pattern of Jesus himself, who crossed every boundary to embrace the outcast.

Let our voice be kind as it is clear, calling ourselves—and our nation—toward repentance for ways we have failed the stranger, and urging one another to take up the Kingdom work of welcome.

In a world torn by suspicion and division, let our lives be living testaments to the Gospel, marked by hospitality, empathy, and the conviction that what we have done for the least, we have done for our Savior. May God give us wisdom, courage, and grace to welcome others as Christ has welcomed us—and in so doing, to inherit the joy prepared for us since the foundation of the world[2][1].
